Enumeration & Verification

SmallCats is built by writing down every category with $n$ morphisms and $k$ objects, once each. The interesting word there is "once": the obvious approach produces each category hundreds or thousands of times over, in every possible labelling, and then has to throw the duplicates away. We don't do that any more. Here's how the search avoids producing a duplicate in the first place, how we check the answer afterwards, and a note about why two of our counts differ from those reported by Cruttwell and Leblanc.

Disclaimer This is a mostly-AI-written explanation of the algorithm.

Representing a category by a table

Fix nonnegative integers $n$ and $k$. Let $T(n,k)$ be the number of isomorphism classes of categories with $n$ morphisms and $k$ objects. The morphisms include the $k$ identities, so necessarily $k\leq n$. We count categories up to isomorphism, not merely up to equivalence, and take $T(0,0)=1$ for the empty category.

We label the morphisms by $M$, reserving the subset $O$ for the identities:

$$ M=\{0,\dots,n-1\}, \qquad O=\{0,\dots,k-1\}\subseteq M. $$

Here each object is identified with its identity morphism. For $f\in M$, write $d(f)$ and $c(f)$ for the identity morphisms of its domain and codomain; in particular, $d(i)=c(i)=i$ for $i\in O$. We store composition in an $n\times n$ table $A$, using the extra value $n$ to mean that a composite is undefined:

$$ A_{f,g}= \begin{cases} f\circ g, & d(f)=c(g),\\ n, & d(f)\neq c(g). \end{cases} $$

Our convention is that $f\circ g$ means “first $g$, then $f$.” The identity and associativity axioms become the constraints

$$ A_{f,d(f)}=f=A_{c(f),f} $$

and

$$ A_{A_{f,g},h}=A_{f,A_{g,h}} $$

whenever $d(f)=c(g)$ and $d(g)=c(h)$. Together with the domain, codomain, and composability constraints, these are exactly the category axioms in multiplication-table form. So a category with $n$ morphisms and $k$ objects is just a table satisfying finitely many constraints, and enumerating categories means enumerating those tables.

The trouble with labels

One category usually appears under many labellings. The $k$ identity morphisms may be permuted among themselves, and the other $n-k$ morphisms may be permuted among themselves, so the relevant relabelling group is

$$ G_{n,k}=S_k\times S_{n-k}, $$

where $S_r$ is the symmetric group on $r$ labels. Extend each $\sigma\in G_{n,k}$ by setting $\sigma(n)=n$, so that the undefined value stays put. The relabelled table is

$$ (\sigma\cdot A)_{f,g} =\sigma\!\left(A_{\sigma^{-1}(f),\sigma^{-1}(g)}\right), $$

and two tables describe isomorphic categories exactly when they lie in the same orbit of this action.

Now, $\lvert G_{n,k}\rvert$ is enormous: it's $4{,}320$ for $(9,3)$ and $17{,}280$ for $(10,4)$. If we just ask a solver for every table satisfying the axioms, we get back up to that many copies of each category, and we're left holding hundreds of millions of tables that we have to sort out afterwards. That is how this database used to be built. It works, but it's slow, it produces enormous intermediate files, and every step of the deduplication is a chance to get the group wrong and lose a category. So instead we arrange for the search to produce one table per isomorphism class and no more.

Fixing the shape first

Here's the key observation. Forget composition for a moment and keep only the domains and codomains: what's left is a directed multigraph on the $k$ objects, with one edge for each of the $n-k$ nonidentity morphisms. Call its isomorphism class the shape of the category.

Every category has exactly one shape. And if we fix a shape and consider only categories with that shape, then two of them are isomorphic exactly when some automorphism of the shape carries one to the other — a relabelling that changed the shape would have landed us somewhere else entirely. So if we enumerate the shapes first, one per isomorphism class, the group we still have to worry about shrinks from $G_{n,k}$ all the way down to $\operatorname{Aut}(\text{shape})$, which is usually trivial.

Fixing the shape buys us something else too. Once the domains and codomains are pinned down, the entry $A_{f,g}$ can only be a morphism with the domain of $g$ and the codomain of $f$ — that is, an element of a particular hom-set, which for these sizes usually has one or two elements in it. Every cell of the table starts out with a very short list of possibilities.

Filling in the table

With a shape fixed, we fill in the table by backtracking, checking associativity as we go. The order we fill the cells in is what makes the duplicates go away, so it's worth being precise about it: we fill in cells by level, where level $d$ means the row and column of morphism $d$. Filling in level $d$ completes the $(d+1)\times(d+1)$ square in the top left corner, so at every stage the part of the table we've written down is a square subtable.

That's the point. Whenever we finish a level, we can take any automorphism of the shape that maps that square to itself, apply it, and compare the result with what we have. If it comes out smaller, we're looking at a table that some other branch of the search will find in its least form, and we can abandon this branch immediately. If it comes out larger, that automorphism can never make anything smaller later on either, so we drop it and stop checking it. If it ties, we keep it and check again at the next level.

At the last level the square is the whole table and every automorphism maps it to itself, so every automorphism still in play gets compared. What survives is the least table in its $\operatorname{Aut}(\text{shape})$ orbit — and there is exactly one of those per orbit. No deduplication pass, no canonical form to compute, no enormous intermediate file. Easy enough!

Not searching for disconnected categories

We only ever run that search for connected categories. A disconnected category is a disjoint union of smaller connected ones, and that decomposition is unique, so a disconnected category is exactly a multiset of strictly smaller connected categories — all of which we already have. Rather than searching for them we assemble them, which is both far cheaper and, as we'll see below, a check on the arithmetic. In the $(9,3)$ cell this means searching for $3{,}568$ categories instead of $60{,}322$.

Counting connected components

Form a graph whose vertices are the objects of a category. Each nonidentity morphism $f\colon x\to y$ supplies an undirected edge between $x$ and $y$. We call the category connected when this graph is connected. Every finite category is uniquely a disjoint union of connected categories.

Let $C(n,k)$ be the number of connected categories with $n$ morphisms and $k$ objects. Since a general category is a finite multiset of connected categories, we have the formal power-series identity

$$ \sum_{n\geq 0}\sum_{k=0}^{n}T(n,k)x^ny^k = \prod_{n\geq 1}\prod_{k=1}^{n} \left(1-x^ny^k\right)^{-C(n,k)}. $$

Here is why. One fixed connected category with $n$ morphisms and $k$ objects may appear zero times, once, twice, and so on. Its possible contributions are

$$ 1+x^ny^k+x^{2n}y^{2k}+\cdots =\frac{1}{1-x^ny^k}. $$

There are $C(n,k)$ connected isomorphism classes of that size, which gives the exponent $C(n,k)$. Multiplying over all sizes constructs every finite multiset of connected categories, and hence every finite category.

Consequently, the connected counts in smaller cells determine the number $D(n,k)$ of disconnected categories before we inspect the $(n,k)$ file:

$$ D(n,k)= [x^ny^k] \prod_{a=1}^{n-1}\prod_{b=1}^{a} \left(1-x^ay^b\right)^{-C(a,b)}, \qquad T(n,k)=C(n,k)+D(n,k). $$

We check more than the final coefficient. If a component size $(a,b)$ occurs $r$ times and there are $c=C(a,b)$ connected categories of that size, then there are $\binom{c+r-1}{r}$ ways to choose those components. Multiplying over the distinct component sizes gives the exact number of categories with that component pattern. Every disconnected component pattern in every populated cell occurs with the expected multiplicity.

This calculation determines the disconnected part of a cell, but it cannot detect a missing connected category: that still requires the exhaustive search or a separate argument. This distinction is exactly what we need for the two disputed cells.

Where our counts differ from Cruttwell-Leblanc

Morphisms Objects Cruttwell-Leblanc SmallCats

9 morphisms and 3 objects

Start with the part that does not depend on the search in this cell. The smaller connected counts give

$$ D(9,3)=56{,}754. $$

The original search finds $C(9,3)=3{,}568$ connected categories. Therefore

$$ T(9,3)=56{,}754+3{,}568=60{,}322. $$

The Cruttwell–Leblanc total would instead imply

$$ C(9,3)=60{,}201-56{,}754=3{,}447, $$

so their total has a connected contribution $121$ smaller than ours. Their presentation says that this is where their program changed to counting connected categories and reconstructing the disconnected ones, making that stage the natural place to investigate. Without their code or intermediate output, however, we cannot distinguish an error in the connected search from an error in the reconstruction or reporting.

It's worth saying clearly that this is a local disagreement and not a general one. We have since enumerated the $(9,2)$ cell as well, and we get $T(9,2)=1{,}868{,}157$ — exactly their number, and the only value in that row nobody had checked. Their $T(9,4)$, $T(9,5)$ and $T(9,6)$ agree with us too, as do $T(10,5)$ and $T(10,6)$. The two cells we disagree about, $(9,3)$ and $(10,4)$, are precisely the two largest connected searches their method had to perform, which is what you would expect from a search that stopped early rather than from a mistake in the reasoning.

10 morphisms and 4 objects

Here the reported value $65{,}922$ cannot satisfy the component formula. Even if we use Cruttwell and Leblanc’s lower value at $(9,3)$, their smaller counts produce

$$ D_{\mathrm{CL}}(10,4)=67{,}392>65{,}922. $$

There would already be more disconnected categories than their proposed total. Using the corrected value $T(9,3)=60{,}322$ instead gives

$$ D(10,4)=67{,}513. $$

It remains to count the connected categories. An exhaustive search gives $1{,}465$ connected skeletal categories, meaning that no two distinct objects are isomorphic.

Every nonskeletal category is obtained from its skeleton $S$ by replacing a skeleton object $x$ with $m_x$ isomorphic copies. The resulting category $\mathcal C$ has

$$ \sum_x m_x=4, \qquad |\operatorname{Mor}(\mathcal C)| =\sum_{x,y\in\operatorname{Ob}(S)} m_xm_y\,|\operatorname{Hom}_S(x,y)|. $$

If the skeleton has one object, then that object is copied four times, and its identity morphism alone produces $4^2=16$ morphisms. If the skeleton has two objects, their multiplicities are either $(3,1)$ or $(2,2)$. The identity morphisms contribute $3^2+1^2=10$ or $2^2+2^2=8$ morphisms, and connectedness requires at least one morphism between the two objects, contributing another $3$ or $4$. Thus the two-object cases have at least $13$ or $12$ morphisms.

A nonskeletal category in the $(10,4)$ cell must therefore have a three-object skeleton with multiplicities $(2,1,1)$. Such a connected skeleton has at least five morphisms. Duplicating one object adds three identity-derived morphisms and at least one copy of an incident morphism, so it adds at least four altogether. A skeleton which expands to exactly ten morphisms can consequently have only five or six morphisms.

We now filter the complete $(5,3)$ and $(6,3)$ lists to their connected skeletal entries, mark each possible object to duplicate, and quotient those marked choices by automorphisms of the skeleton. Exactly $12$ isomorphism classes remain.

Hence

$$ C(10,4)=1{,}465+12=1{,}477 $$

and finally

$$ \begin{aligned} T(10,4) &=D(10,4)+C(10,4)\\ &=67{,}513+1{,}477\\ &=68{,}990. \end{aligned} $$

Checking the answer

A search that promises not to repeat itself is only worth as much as the check on that promise, so here are the checks we actually run.

The first is the direct one. For each stored cell we take every table, compute the least relabelling of it under the full group $G_{n,k}$ — the canonical form we deliberately avoid computing during the search — and look for two tables that produce the same one. Finding none means no two lines of the file describe the same category. We also confirm that every line really is a category: identities behave, composites are defined exactly when the domains and codomains agree, and every associativity triple holds.

The second is the component formula above, which predicts the disconnected part of a cell before we build any of it. It's a sharper test than the totals suggest, because it also predicts how many categories have each pattern of component sizes. Every disconnected category in every populated cell turns up with exactly the multiplicity it should.

The third applies only to the monoids, and comes from outside this project entirely. A finite monoid whose only unit is the identity is exactly a semigroup with an identity adjoined, and that correspondence is a bijection, so the number of monoids of order $n$ minus the number of semigroups of order $n-1$ has to be the number of monoids of order $n$ with a nontrivial group of units. The semigroups of order seven have been counted independently of anything here — there are $1{,}627{,}672$ of them (A027851) — and we find $1{,}668{,}997$ monoids of order eight, of which exactly $41{,}325$ have a nontrivial unit group. The difference comes out right, and nothing about our search went into the semigroup count.
